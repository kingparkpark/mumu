# Seedance shot director

## Direct each scene before you split shots

- Write a director statement for each scene before you split it into shots.

- Name the tone of the scene in concrete terms, not with a genre label alone.

- Trace the emotional curve from its opening feeling to its closing feeling and name the beat where the peak falls.

- Name each laugh, tear, or scare point by beat number and name the means that makes it work, such as situation, performance, lens, or sound.

- Name the visual motif of the scene or state plainly that it has none and name what carries it instead.

- Choose the main focal length and the light direction and quality from the six focal decision tree and say why they support the tone.

- Write one intent sentence at the top of every shot that says what the audience should feel and what makes the shot work.

- Reject any shot whose intent is only a plot retelling and rewrite it until the intent names a feeling or an information point.

- Log every director addition on its own line in the shot, and write none when you added nothing.

- Keep every director addition out of the numbered beats and never let an addition replace, push aside, or rewrite a locked beat or line.

## Break the script into generation units

- Split a script into complete generation units before you write any prompt.

- Split action at a completed result and carry that result into the next unit.

- Keep each line of dialogue whole inside one shot and never split one line across shots.

- Respect a user request for a shot count or for one long take and do not override it with a default split.

- List the beats each shot covers by number, one by one, and attach a coverage table that maps every beat to a shot.

- Never merge two beats into one action and never replace individual reactions with a group reaction.

- Keep every beat the script wrote and mark any uncertain beat with a question for a person instead of deleting it.

## Diagnose each shot before you write it

- List the characters present, the setting, the props, the action, the aspect ratio, the camera mode, the spatial positions and the light source for each shot.

- Remove characters who are absent, expired plot labels, scene numbers and literary filler from the prompt.

- Block a shot that opens on an empty frame or brings a character in late.

- Block axis jumps, reversed left and right positions and confused facing directions.

- Block focal drift, flat frontal light, duplicated characters and props that vanish or change hands without cause.

- Write dialogue exactly as written in the script and write that a silent character keeps the lips closed and still.

## Direct space, lens, camera, and performance

- Place every important element with an absolute physical anchor such as a distance from an edge, a foot on a named step or a hand on a named object.

- Avoid vague placement words such as nearby or somewhere.

- Use a 47 degree standard lens for two person dialogue and mid shot narrative at 3 to 5 meters.

- Use an 84 degree wide lens for action tension and spatial explanation at 1 to 1.5 meters.

- Use a 107 degree corrected ultra wide lens for a pressing subjective view at 0.5 to 0.8 meters.

- Use a 29 degree short portrait lens for face close ups and psychological beats at 4 to 6 meters.

- Use an 18 degree long lens for compressed isolation at 6 to 8 meters.

- Use an 8 degree ultra long lens for observed or epic scale scenes at 20 to 25 meters.

- Choose the focal length for each shot from the decision tree and give its reason, and never run a whole scene on one default focal length.

- Order each prompt from scene context to active reference to place mapping to first frame positions to character anchors to format to lens to camera position to action timeline to physics to light to audio to positive constraints to negative constraints.

- Lock light before you polish style and keep the light direction consistent.

- Use only one camera movement in a shot and never combine push, pull, pan, tilt and lift in one shot.

- Write every key action with its speed, its force and its exact timing, because the same action noun can read as opposite meanings without them.

- Call the local acting video character skill for per shot performance and give each character in the shot a goal verb aimed at a partner, a current strategy, and a hand task.

- Name the expression for each face in the shot with its number and intensity when the project has an expression table, and write each channel of that expression into the visible detail.

- Give each shot a visible trigger for any smile or laugh in the same shot, following the smile trigger rule in the acting skill.

## Carry state across cuts and deliver self contained segments

- Carry costume, injuries, marks, weather, light direction and eye direction unchanged across every cut.

- Carry character positions, weapon hands and scene damage from the end of one clip into the start of the next clip.

- Write prompts mainly in Chinese and add precise bilingual cinematography terms for focal length, aperture and camera movement.

- Treat each finished prompt as a standalone file that states everything visible and audible in that shot.

- Never write that a shot continues an earlier shot or is the same as before, and restate every needed state inside the shot itself.

- List only the references that this segment actually uses, never carry an earlier segment reference forward, and never invent a reference label.

- Spend prompt words on control items such as identity anchors, spatial scheduling, first frame, eye direction, landmark distance, hand state, prop state, timed action, optical and light locks, physical rules, and dialogue.

- Keep background people, unrelated costume detail, inactive props, and anything already clear in a reference image brief.

- Write each shot duration in whole seconds or half seconds only and never use 0.1 second precision.

- Calculate spoken seconds as word count divided by 4.5 and add the seconds of action that cannot overlap the speech.

- Group shots by one generation capacity, about one beat and about 10 to 15 seconds, and give each group its own global block, negative prompt, and references so it can be generated on its own.

- Deliver the shot list and the prompts in clear blocks ready to paste into a generation tool without method explanation.

## Alternatives for better results

- Use one continuous take when a scene gains its effect from unbroken movement in one space.

- Start from an existing art asset when one exists and read its appearance into the first shot instead of inventing a look.

- Keep a dialogue scene in fewer wider shots when the dialogue itself carries the scene instead of cutting on every line.
